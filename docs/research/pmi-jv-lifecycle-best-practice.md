## Area A — Post-merger integration governance (deal close → value realization)

Context: integration, not deal selection, is where value is lost — "when deals fail, integration is at the root 83% of the time" [A1]. Cross-firm consensus (McKinsey/BCG/Bain/Galpin): start planning at diligence, run a dedicated IMO under a steering committee, decide people early, treat culture as a design choice, track synergies monthly with named owners, then formally hand over to BAU.

### A.1 Pre-close (signing → close)
- Appoint a single integration leader and stand up the Integration Management Office (IMO) at signing, staffed from both companies. [A5][A6]
- Write an integration thesis derived from the deal thesis: how each value driver will be captured (scale deals consolidate fast; scope deals integrate selectively). [A1][A6]
- Charter a clean team (third-party specialists and reassignable employees) under legal protocols agreed by both legal departments, operating behind a firewall, for pre-close work on sensitive data. [A3]
- Restrict clean-team scope to genuinely sensitive, high-value topics (procurement overlap, customer overlap/migration, product-roadmap harmonization); keep the rest of planning in functional teams to preserve ownership. [A3]
- Set top-down stretch synergy targets (steering committee + IMO), then validate bottom-up by the leaders who must deliver them — BCG's "W" approach. [A3]
- Filter synergy initiatives through five stage gates: ideas → quantified ranges with assumptions/risks → vetted plans with sign-off by delivery owners and steering committee → implementation embedded in budgets → impact accrual. [A3]
- Build functional integration hypotheses during diligence: finance (close process, controls, chart of accounts), HR (key talent, retention risk, leadership overlap), IT (application map, cutover, cyber), commercial (account concentration, cross-sell realism), operations (footprint, supply chain), legal (change-of-control clauses, consents). [A6]
- Identify key talent during diligence; agree retention packages and reporting lines before close; decide which manager appointments are announced on Day 1. [A6]
- Establish the revenue baseline (premerger forecast absent the deal) so revenue synergies are attributable. [A3]
- Size revenue synergies down to product and customer level; prepare customer factbooks and migration plans pre-close. [A1][A3]
- Assess cultural "fault lines" (purpose, decision making, engagement) during diligence using outside-in data and pre-close surveys/interviews. [A2]
- Plan for multiple closing scenarios: challenged deals now take ~3 months longer, and >40% take over a year to clear regulators — use stage-gated planning so the base business keeps running. [A1]
- Decide the day-one interim operating model separately from the end-state model; interim models commonly run one to two years, especially in commercial units where pre-close data sharing is restricted. [A4]
- Complete a Day-1 readiness checklist tracked early: signing/payment authority, payroll and banking access, customer/supplier communications, systems and site access, statutory filings, delegation-of-authority matrix, safety/compliance controls. [A5]

### A.2 Day 1
- Treat Day 1 as a continuity milestone, not a victory lap — the objective is containing confusion. [A6]
- Announce the top two to three leadership layers; "set yourself an ambitious deadline for filling the top levels, and stick to it." [A1]
- Launch the agreed interim (day-one) operating model with clear reporting lines. [A4]
- Execute pre-planned customer and supplier communications (clean-team-prepared portfolio decisions ready by day 1 to reassure customers and limit attrition). [A3]
- Activate the synergy tracker: every initiative has an owner, baseline, timing, dependency map, and financial impact. [A6]
- Confirm control handover: treasury authority, statutory filings, DoA matrix, business-continuity triggers.

### A.3 First 100 days
- Run the standing cadence: weekly workstream stand-ups, weekly IMO escalation meeting, monthly steering committee, quarterly board reporting for the first 100 days. [A6]
- Put the handful of pivotal cross-functional decisions (cross-sell model, org design, footprint) on the steering-committee agenda and resolve them early. [A1]
- Use the IMO as a "culture lab" to surface frictions and test explicit decision-making norms; run a perceptions workshop to put cultural issues on the table. [A1][A2]
- Compare promised vs. actual synergy value every month; escalate late items, unowned workstreams, and unfunded day-1 dependencies. [A6]
- Stand up revenue-synergy enablement: sales training on new products/segments, clear account-ownership rules, KPI tracking including lead generation, year-one cross-sell incentives. [A3]
- Track attrition of identified key talent at 30/60/90 days and re-engage; retention planning quality "determines how much talent leakage the buyer suffers in the first ninety days." [A6]
- Fold approved synergy initiatives into line budgets so plans "become integrated business roadmaps, not isolated cost-cutting exercises." [A3]

### A.4 Through exit (value-up phase → BAU handover)
- Keep integration governance in place until: synergy run-rate is certified by Finance, the end-state operating model layers are announced, retention milestones have passed, and remaining initiatives are embedded in line budgets. [A3][A6]
- Formally demobilize the IMO; no open item transfers to BAU without a named line executive owner and a date. [A6]
- Publish final synergy accounting; BCG research links disciplined tracking and investor communication to superior market performance. [A3]
- Run a deal post-mortem/lookback; update the M&A playbook, templates, and the trained-integration-manager bench (repeatable model). [A1][A7]
- Codify learning into the next deal's diligence checklist — programmatic acquirers win on "tacit knowledge" and "playbooks." [A7]

### A.5 Standing bodies and decision rights
- Steering committee (executive sponsors): approves synergy targets, stage-gate sign-offs, pivotal decisions, escalations. [A1][A3]
- IMO: creates the comprehensive plan, manages risks and interdependencies, "sets governance, escalation rules, and decision rights," and tracks whether promised value "is actually appearing in the numbers"; standard tools: IMO charter, RACI, workstream plans, communication plan, risk register, synergy tracker. [A5][A6]
- Integration manager profile: "credibility with the executive team, strong project management expertise," and "persuasion, empathy, negotiation, diplomacy." [A5]
- Workstream leads from both companies: finance, HR, IT, operations, commercial, legal, communications. [A6]
- Synergy owners: one accountable executive per initiative. [A3][A6]
- Escalation path: workstream lead → IMO weekly issue log → steering committee → CEO/board, with decision SLAs. [A5][A6]

### A.6 Documented failure modes
- Integration is the root cause of 83% of deal failures. [A1]
- Nearly half of practitioners cite cultural fit / management-team integration difficulty as a primary cause of failed deals; 75% struggle with culture requiring serious intervention. [A2]
- Revenue-synergy shortfall is "one of the most common causes of deal failure"; only half of executives get enough granularity on revenue synergies. [A1]
- Slow people decisions drive talent flight: until appointments are announced, best customers and employees "will be actively poached." [A1]
- Mismatched decision rights/norms between merging companies slow integration unless addressed early. [A6]
- Gun-jumping risk: EC fines up to 10% of revenue (Illumina/Grail) — sensitive data only via clean-team protocol. [A3]

## Area B — JV lifecycle beyond formation (operate, monitor, restructure, exit)

Framing (Ankura/Watermark): formation → launch → operate → restructure/exit. Median JV lifespan is 10 years and 65% of JV endings are partner buyouts [B2]. Evolution is the success factor: "JVs that undergo at least one major restructuring were more than twice as likely (79% vs. 33%) to meet their owners' strategic and financial objectives" [B3][B4].

### B.1 Operate — governance cadence
- Maintain a board-approved JV governance framework — only 45% of JVs have one; median governance score across 101 studied JVs was 5.4/10 and 41% scored "weak." [B1]
- Limit board meeting attendance to actual directors (no back-benchers); rebalance board time from operations toward strategy and talent. [B1]
- Cadence: quarterly JV board meetings plus one annual board strategy off-site. [B1][B4]
- Link annual objectives to director roles; run periodic board/director self-assessments; provide JV-specific director training. [B1]
- Maintain a reserved-matters map (board vs. shareholder decisions); expand reserved shareholder matters to move conflict-prone decisions out of the board. [B5]
- Operate a conflict-of-interest protocol covering the three JV conflict types — business opportunity, self-dealing, information disclosure — and choose the fiduciary-duty approach (explicit affirmation / implicit / qualified waiver / complete waiver) deliberately. [B5]
- Stand up a Shareholders Committee (senior business sponsors) for major strategy/investment decisions and a Commercial Committee for shareholder-JV supply and purchase agreements. [B5]
- Keep parent-JV commercial relationships at arm's-length pricing. [B4]
- Apply three-lines-of-defense risk management adapted to JVs. [B8]

### B.2 Monitor — JV health KPIs (per-JV scorecard)
- Performance: a one-sentence definition of success plus financial/operating KPIs vs. business plan. [B3]
- People: JV employee engagement and retention (only ~50% of JV employees report positive culture). [B4]
- Partner alignment: agreement on long-term strategy (69% of partners are at odds) and on annual budgets (58% cannot agree). [B4]
- Decision health: decision cycle time, repeated board reviews of the same matter, deadlock frequency, escalation instances. [B4][B6]
- Milestones and funding: strategic milestones met, capital calls vs. plan, parent capability delivery vs. commitments. [B4]
- Governance health: director tenure (median 30 months), days per year on governance (15), framework existence, self-assessment completion. [B1][B3]
- Value distribution: symmetry of economics across partners (asymmetric economics is a leading failure cause). [B4]

Parent portfolio-level:
- Annual strategic asset review per JV challenging strategic fit, partner alignment, and performance (SABIC practice). [B3]
- Legal gap analysis vs. market norms; governance-practice assessment; internal accountability audit; talent indicators for JV directors/asset managers/secondees. [B3]
- Independent external governance review every three years; regular JV reporting to executive leadership and board (BHP practice). [B3]

### B.3 Warning signs triggering deeper review
- Extended decision cycles requiring multiple board reviews; competing parent guidance to management. [B4]
- Product bloat serving both owners instead of customers; loss of entrepreneurial spirit under corporate processes. [B4]
- Parents failing to deliver promised capabilities/technology; venture structure outdated vs. market. [B4]
- Baseline expectation: at least half of JVs fail on one or more owner objectives — set formal restructuring triggers on metrics rather than waiting for crisis. [B4]
- Timing bias: the median JV takes 39 months longer to restructure than a comparable wholly-owned business — start restructuring conversations early. [B3]

### B.4 Restructure — options when underperforming
- Formal restructuring triggers tied to metrics (performance thresholds, milestone misses, deadlock). [B4]
- Restructuring menu: renegotiate scope or ownership; change governance (board composition, reserved matters, decision rights); rebalance economics (pricing, fees, contributions); shift the operating model (independence ↔ interdependence); leadership change; ownership change. [B3][B4]
- Pre-agree evolution paths for ownership and scope changes in the JV agreement ("inability to evolve" is failure cause #9). [B4]
- Use Key Learning Landmarks (KLLs) with financial incentives and parent-endorsed learning agendas to force structured evolution. [B4]
- Deadlock ladder: functional managers (15–30 days) → senior executives with authority to commit resources (30–60 days) → CEOs → institutional mediation; each stage time-boxed and documented. [B6]
- Assign a clear internal owner for restructuring work — it is typically orphaned between business units and corporate strategy. [B3]

### B.5 Exit — paths and governance
Exit design begins at formation (Pyle & Bamford five steps) [B2]:
1. Review strategic considerations: desired end game (long-term partnership, staged exit, new partners, IPO stepping-stone); identify the "natural buyer" and "natural seller."
2. Define exit triggers — 16 categories exist; most agreements use 4–8: partner breach (~67% of agreements), bankruptcy/insolvency, change of control, deadlock/dispute (25%), ownership below threshold, performance misses/milestones, force majeure, term expiry. At-will exit rights in 60% of agreements (98% of those restricted, typically ROFR/ROFO); lock-ups in 25% (commonly 5 years).
3. Select mechanisms: put, call, buy-sell, liquidation rights, third-party sale rights; protections for the non-triggering party: ROFR (30–60 day match window), ROFO, tag-along (minority), drag-along (majority). [B2][B6]
4. Choose valuation: negotiated market price / pre-agreed formula / independent appraisal (two-appraisers-then-third process) / auction; premium-discount modifiers in 32% of agreements (most commonly 10%), plus caps/floors. [B2][B6]
5. Plan post-exit transition across six areas: IP licensing, people/secondees, services, supply & purchase arrangements, non-competes, debt/guarantees. [B2]

Exit execution paths: sale to partner (65% of endings), sale to third party, IPO, unwind/dissolution. [B2]
Dissolution/wind-down: appointed wind-up manager/committee; settle obligations and regulatory filings; distribute per priority provisions; dissolution reserves; post-dissolution tax/compliance tail. [B6]
Exit decision governance: shareholder-level decision per the agreement; internal alignment working session (business, strategy, legal) before exercising rights. [B2]

### B.6 Parent-side JV management
- Designate executive accountability for JVs (38% of companies; CFO/GC/Head of Strategy/CRO/VP-JV) with a supporting team (0.5–40+ FTE). [B3]
- Avoid "corporate adhocracy" (36% of companies; lowest practice adoption). [B3]
- Consider a JV affairs unit with SABIC's threefold mandate: (1) corporate JV standards, (2) venture-management support, (3) transaction structuring/diligence support. [B3]
- For non-operated JVs: dedicated asset teams, annual influencing plans, standards plus audits (BHP "Asset President of Non-Operated JVs" model). [B3]
- Maintain a JV portfolio gameboard by venture type and lifecycle phase: non-operated production assets, infrastructure JVs, restructuring/exit, transactions/diligence, operated JVs, technology partnerships, corporate strategic partners. [B3]
- Run post-close transaction lookbacks feeding standards updates. [B3]

## Area C — Corporate venture / new-business portfolio monitoring

Context: standard CVC success rates are ~20–30% (similar to or slightly below VC); a disciplined venture-building model achieved 66%; typical corporate portfolios average ~15% incubations, ~40% early-stage investments, ~45% later-stage [C1]. Bifurcate metrics: financial (IRR vs. hurdle rate, cash-on-cash multiples) and strategic (deals prospected, BU–startup introductions, learning quality progressing to tangible product development/partnerships/JVs) [C2].

### C.1 Portfolio KPIs
- Classify every venture by stage and vehicle type (incubation, minority investment, majority, standalone build). [C1][C3]
- Hold a scaling blueprint per venture: ambition, milestones, metrics, each side's contributions, and governance. [C3]
- Mix financial metrics (revenue growth, customer lifetime value) with strategic metrics (customer count, market share, markets entered). [C3]
- Track readiness-to-scale characteristics: momentum, strong team, customer focus, USP, clear growth vision. [C3]
- Portfolio level: stage-progression rate, capital deployed vs. milestone plan, valuation marks by stage normalized for time in market, kill/graduate decision counts. [C1][C3]
- Review strategic KPIs from leading (learning, introductions) to lagging (products, partnerships, JVs). [C2]
- Hold ventures to benchmark odds: 20–30% success is normal for CVC — continued funding beyond that requires explicit evidence. [C1]

### C.2 Milestone-gated tranche funding
- No tranche releases without blueprint milestones, metrics, and documented contribution responsibilities from both parent and venture. [C3]
- Route gate decisions through a small, senior investment committee for speed, with integrity guardrails (data, reputation, legal/regulatory compliance). [C1]
- Maintain a scaling and communication office as the decision gateway: tracks milestones and achievements, maintains the link to the corporate parent. [C3]
- Track parent-side capability commitments (capabilities gap analysis; shared services such as legal) as obligations. [C3]
- Enforce "freedom within financial guardrails": parent agrees not to interfere with day-to-day operations while the venture pursues agreed growth goals. [C3]
- Run a change management and capability-building office: talent planning, transparency to both organizations, integration concept preparation if the venture is absorbed. [C3]

### C.3 Escalation, kill, and graduation governance
- Diagnose starting position and concentrate resources only on the most promising ventures for growth. [C3]
- At each stage transition, evaluate the exit path against a clear rationale: integrate into a business unit; standalone unit within the parent; spinout; reduce ownership with new strategic partners; divest — or kill the venture if unsuccessful and divestment is not possible. [C3]
- Set explicit review windows: ventures that do not demonstrate measurable impact quickly lose management support — time-box evidence requirements. [C3]
- Record kill decisions with rationale and learning capture; recycle insights into the venture playbook. [C1][C3]
- Prepare the graduation/integration concept before absorbing a venture into the core. [C3]
- Benchmark the portfolio's valuation distribution (on-track vs. outperform mix) normalized for time in market as a deliberate strategy choice. [C1]
